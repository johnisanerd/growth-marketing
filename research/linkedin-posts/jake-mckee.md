**Title:** Stop giving away the best part
**Type:** Article
**Likes:** 3
**URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/stop-giving-away-best-part-jake-mckee-dqmpc
**Content:**

I have been a scale modeler, a photographer, and a woodworker my whole life. I was crafting as a toddler and never stopped. With a history like that, you would think I would be really, really good by now. Maybe I am. But what I know for sure is that I still have a lot to learn. A lot. In today's grind culture, there are plenty of reasons that I might have given up instead of continuing to drive forward. Either I was not great at them right away, or I could not see a quick payoff for the effort. So why do I keep going? The simple answer is the true one. I love the craft itself. I love making something interesting out of a pile of raw materials. I love the way the workshop smells. I love the conversations that happen before, during, and after, with other people who make things too. The joy comes out of the effort. And that joy is what pushes me to keep going, spend more time, get better, and see what I have been missing. And that joy is what drives completed, award winning projects. It turns out this is not just me. A 2023 study of about 93,000 people across 16 countries found that those with hobbies reported more happiness and higher life satisfaction. The researchers tied a lot of that to sensory engagement. The hands in the clay. The smell of the wood. The stuff you can only feel by doing. That is the heart of any craft. The skill lives in the contact. A woodworker runs a hand along a board before they cut it. They can feel the grain. They know which way it wants to split and which way it wants to hold. A cook tastes the sauce while it cooks, adjusting as it simmers. A potter feels the clay shift under their fingers and knows when it is about to give. None of these people work from a distance. The hands on the wood. The spoon in the pot. That direct touch is where the best work comes from. The direct touch is the very craft itself. It is also where the joy comes from. Ask a woodworker why they love it and they will not talk about the finished table. They will talk about the smell of the shavings and the moment a joint fits perfectly. Craft and your product team Product teams too often treat customer conversation like a chore, something to hand off. We build a research team, or we hire a research firm, or we send out a survey, and we tell ourselves we are being smart with our time. Let the experts handle it. We will read the report. But conversation is a craft too. And we just gave away the part where our own hands touch the work. When you talk to a customer yourself, you feel things a report can never carry. You hear the pause before they answer. You catch the thing they almost said and then stopped themselves. You notice they lit up about a feature you thought was boring, and went flat on the one you were proud of. That is the grain of the wood. That is the taste of the sauce. There is a reason this matters so much. The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi spent his career studying flow, that feeling of being so deep in something that time slips away. One thing flow needs is immediate feedback. A musician in flow hears right away whether they are hitting the right notes, and adjusts on the spot. A real conversation works the same way. You ask, they answer, you feel the room shift, and you follow it. A report cannot give you that. By the time it reaches you, the feedback loop is no longer a loop..it’s a broadcast. Hand it off, and someone else feels all of that.You get a clean summary with all the life squeezed out of it. You get the table, but you felt the joy of that joint sliding cleanly, perfectly, smoothly into place. You lose the since of connection and accomplishment to the work. You did not just lose a big part of the insight. You lost the joy too. In a craft, those two things are the same thing. The contact that makes the work good is the same contact that makes it fun. You cannot keep one and give away the other. I am not saying fire your research team. A great chef still has a kitchen full of people. Someone preps the vegetables, someone washes the pans, someone takes the food to the table. But the chef still tastes the food. That part never gets handed off, because that part is the job. Your research team can do a lot. They can run the big studies, find patterns across hundreds of people, and handle the heavy lifting you cannot do alone. But the moment you stop tasting the food yourself, you stop being a chef and start being a manager who reads reviews of their own kitchen. Reclaiming your craft mindset When someone tells me they want to start scale modeling, or photography, or woodworking, my advice is always the same. Just start. Pick something that looks interesting and jump in. Start something, anything. It won’t be great, and that is fine. Do not think about the outcome. Be proud that you started at all. Your first results will be wonky. Hell, you might not even finish. So what? Do not get caught up in that noise. Find the one or two things that went right and look at those. Then go again. The second try already feels better than the first. And the third will feel nearly miraculous. Customer conversations work exactly the same way. Your first one might be clumsy. You will talk too much, ask a question that goes nowhere, and lead the conversation instead of relaxing into it. But don’t worry, that’s part of it. The first scale model I ever built was rough, and my early photography were worse. Grind culture says quit when you are not good right away. Craft says something else. You get one small result, one moment where a customer says something you never saw coming, and it pulls you back to try again. You have to be focused on finding that moment of value, not self-criticing. Whether in the hobby or customer conversation realm, this is flow at work. As your skill grows, you can take on a little more, and the work stays in that sweet spot where it is not too easy to bore you and not too hard to scare you off. The second conversation goes better than the first. The fifth goes better than the second. At some point you stop forcing yourself to do it. You just can’t wait to do it again. That is the whole secret. The crafters who keep going are not the ones with the most discipline. They are the ones who found the part they love and refused to give it away. **Title:** Listening is the easy part
**Type:** Article
**Likes:** 1
**URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/listening-easy-part-jake-mckee-zdrrc
**Content:**

Will Guidara ran a restaurant called Eleven Madison Park. It went from average to the best restaurant in the world. Not because the food got dramatically better, but because Guidara built a team that actually did something with what they heard from guests. One night he overheard a table talking about their trip to New York. They had eaten everywhere. The best restaurants in the city. But they were leaving the next day and the one thing they had never done was eat a New York street hot dog. Guidara walked out of the restaurant, bought a hot dog from the cart on the corner, brought it back to the kitchen, and had the chef plate it like a fine dining dish. Four small pieces. Ketchup, mustard, sauerkraut, all presented on a literal silver platter. The guests lost their minds. They loved it. But here is the part that matters. Guidara did not keep that story to himself; he told it in the pre-meal staff meeting the next day. The whole team heard it. It became what he called a Legend. And then the team built tool kits so those kinds of moments could happen faster and more often. They created a Legends book. That one act of hospitality became part of the cultural DNA of the restaurant. Something was heard. Someone acted on it. The team validated it. And then it became part of how they operated permanently. Heard. Acted On. Validated. Institutionalized. I recently heard from a product professional when I mentioned that it’s hard to create great products with secondhand insight, he said: “I talk to stakeholders, customers, and users directly all the time. I do not rely on secondhand insights to make investment decisions. Are you saying most product teams do not actually talk to customers?” It is a fair question and one I hear regularly. No. I am not saying most product teams skip customer conversations entirely. Plenty of teams talk to customers. Plenty of leaders have good intentions around it. The problem is subtler and more frustrating than that. I see teams that talk to customers but not on any regular or consistent schedule. Conversations happen in bursts, usually right before a big launch or when something goes wrong, and then go quiet for months. The habit never forms, and the relationships never build. I see teams where one or two people do most of the talking and everyone else makes decisions based on whatever summary those people produce. The raw customer voice never reaches the people who need it most. I see leaders who are genuinely good at having conversations but struggle to turn what they hear into something the team can act on. The insight lives in their head or their notes but never makes it downstream to the designer, the engineer, the person writing the roadmap. I see teams who are convinced that because they talk to customers regularly, they understand them completely. The conversations become a reason to stop questioning rather than a reason to keep digging. Confidence replaces curiosity, and that’s an incredibly dangerous place to be. And most commonly, I see teams that hear something real from a customer, nod at it in a meeting, and then move on. Not because they do not care. Because they have never built the system that turns a good conversation into a changed decision. All of these aren’t listening problems, they are Traction problems. Why action does not follow automatically There is a quiet assumption baked into most product cultures that if you surface a good insight, the right people will hear it and something will change. It feels logical. It almost never works that way. Action does not follow insight. Action follows other action. Specifically, it follows a set of conditions that most teams never build on purpose. Getting to Acted On requires that the insight is connected to a live decision someone is actually making right now, and that it arrives before that window closes. Most insights miss one or both of those conditions. They land in the wrong meeting, at the wrong moment, with no clear owner. So they float. Getting to Validated requires more than a gut feeling that something worked. It means watching for early signals, collecting real evidence, and then pressure testing that evidence before declaring success. Most teams skip this entirely. They act, they move on, and they never actually know if it made a difference. Getting to Institutionalized is where teams fall furthest short. And it is the most important link of all. What Institutionalized actually looks like Guidara celebrated the hot dog story, but more importantly, he baked it into the culture of EMP. He built a ritual around sharing moments like it every single day before service. He documented them in a book that anyone on the team could learn from. He made the behavior structural so it did not depend on him personally showing up and caring about it. That is Institutionalized in action. The practice is embedded deeply enough that it continues when the person who started it leaves the room. Or the company. Getting started with four questions You do not need a framework audit to know where your team stands. Just answer these four questions honestly. Can you name a customer insight your team acted on in the last thirty days? Not something you heard and filed. Something that actually changed a decision or a roadmap item. If you are struggling to name one, that is your starting point. How do you know it worked? What signals did you watch for? What evidence did you collect? If the honest answer is that you assumed it worked, your Validated link needs attention. Where does that story live right now? Could a new team member find it six months from now without asking you? If the answer is somewhere in Slack or in your head, your Institutionalized link is where you are losing ground. If you left your team tomorrow, would any of this continue? If the answer is no, you have not institutionalized anything yet. You have just been doing it yourself. The link where you stumbled first is your work. Guidara's team became the best in the world not because they heard things from guests, but because they built a complete system for taking action with what they heard. Your customers are telling you things that could change your product. The question is not whether you are listening, but whether those conversations are actually driving actions and outcomes. **Title:** Scaffolding: Why struggle is not part of belonging or learning
**Type:** Article
**Likes:** 3
**URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/scaffolding-why-struggle-part-belonging-learning-jake-mckee-tjjec
**Content:**

If you've been following me for a while, you've probably heard me talk about one of my main hobbies: scale modeling. I build small versions of big things. Years ago, when I moved back to Austin, I refreshed my interest in the hobby and found a local club that met once a month. As a community guy, I was eager to show up and nerd out. I brought the first model I'd worked on in many, many years. It wasn't great, but it was complete. And the fact that I'd pushed myself to get back into something that brought me real joy made me proud. One of the first people I met at the club immediately pointed out a historical error on the model. The fact that I can share this story vividly, nearly 20 years later, should tell you something about the impact of that interaction. When we show up somewhere new, without a lot of domain knowledge, how we're treated matters more than most people realize. We want to learn. We want to find our footing. We ask questions like "How do I get started?" or "What are the protocols here?" And sometimes the answer comes back cold: "You have to figure it out yourself." Maybe it's said kindly. Maybe it's wrapped in the…less than desirable… language of learning or earning your way in. But the message is clear: prove your commitment through struggle, or you don't belong here yet. So you leave. Or you stay and feel small. This happens everywhere - hobby communities, online forums, workplaces, product teams. We've built a quiet cultural norm that treats struggle as the price of entry. If it was hard for me, it should be hard for you. That's not rigor. That's gatekeeping. And good teachers don't work that way. Scaffolding as an antidote to gatekeeping In the 1970s, researchers Wood, Bruner, and Ross introduced a concept called scaffolding to describe what good mentors and teachers actually do. It’s a philosophy, or orientation, not a set of specific techniques, per se. A scaffold is what an expert puts in place so someone else can do something they couldn't quite do on their own yet. The goal is always to hand over the work, not to do it for them, and not to leave them stranded. Around the same time, psychologist Lev Vygotsky was describing what he called the Zone of Proximal Development: the gap between what someone can do on their own and what they can do with a little help from someone who knows more. That gap is where real learning happens. Scaffolding is how you help someone work inside that gap without doing the work for them. The connection to everyday conversation is direct. Think about the last time someone asked you how to do something you know well. Maybe it was a coworker asking about a process. A friend asking how you got into your hobby. A new person on your team asking a question you feel like they should already know the answer to. What did you do? If you're honest, you probably did one of two things. You either gave them the full download - everything you know, all at once, whether they were ready for it or not. Or you gave them the brush-off version: "just try it and see" or "it'll make sense once you've done it a few times." Both feel like helping. Neither actually is. There's a third option. It's harder, and most of us skip it without realizing we're doing it. That third option looks like: "Great question. Here are three core protocols most people start with. Once you've got those, come back and we'll go further." Or: "I remember being confused about this too. Here's where I'd start, and here's why." Or even just: "Let me show you where new people usually get stuck." These aren't soft answers. They require more thought than "figure it out" and more restraint than dumping everything you know on someone. They require paying attention to where the other person actually is - and giving them exactly enough to take the next step. That's scaffolding. How to Scaffold in Real Conversation Scaffolding isn't complicated, but it does require a few deliberate habits. Find out where they are first. Before you answer, take a beat and ask one quick question to understand what they already know. "Have you tried X yet?" or "Where are you starting from?" gives you enough to calibrate your answer. You can't scaffold from the right place if you don't know where they're standing. Give the next step, not the whole staircase. Resist the urge to share everything you know. Most people asking a beginner question need one or two things to move forward, not a comprehensive overview. Give them what gets them to the next landing, then stop. Use your own learning as a reference point. "I remember being confused about this" is one of the most powerful things you can say to someone new. It tells them the confusion is normal and that you actually know how to help. It removes shame and opens the door. Leave it open. End your answer with an invitation. "Come back when you've tried that and we'll go further" tells someone they're not a burden and that they're welcome to keep learning here. Notice when someone is overwhelmed and dial back. Sometimes the right scaffold is less, not more. If someone's eyes are glazing over or their replies are getting shorter, you've handed them too much. Pull back and find a smaller next step. And I encourage you to even ask “How are you doing right now?” or “Did I go to far? We can slow down, no problem.” Why We Gatekeep The gatekeeping mindset shows up for a bunch of reasons, and they're not all the same. Sometimes people gatekeep because they're just not kind. They lack the social grace or empathy to think about how their coldness lands on someone else. Sometimes it's about ownership. Someone worked hard to get good at something and they're proud of it. (Maybe too proud!) When we gatekeep, we protect that; we keep it ours instead of watching it become something common that anyone can do. There's a real fear there: if I give this away, what makes me special? Sometimes people gatekeep because they genuinely don't know how to help. They learned through trial and error, and that's the only model they have. They can't imagine scaffolding because nobody scaffolded for them. And sometimes it's a group dynamic thing: the group culture itself doesn't encourage sharing/collaboration. People are taught to protect their techniques, their secrets, their wins… and then it becomes the norm. But underneath a lot of this - especially in Western culture right now - is something deeper. We've become obsessed with control and certainty. We build rules and barriers to make the world feel more predictable and safe. Risk aversion has become our default. And when you're operating from a place of fear and control, connection, sharing, collaboration all feel risky. So we gatekeep instead. We prioritize protecting what we have over welcoming who's arriving. Culture Drives Conversation It’s important to consider that you can teach someone scaffolding techniques all day long, and it won't matter if the group culture doesn't support it. Conversational techniques are always shaped by the culture they exist in. If your hobby club rewards gatekeeping (staying exclusive is what earns respect) then nobody's going to scaffold, no matter how good the technique is, in theory. Same with your product team or your company. The way people actually talk and teach each other is driven by what the group values and what it rewards. So if you want gatekeeping to stop, you have to look at the culture first. You have to decide, as a group, that belonging matters more than exclusivity. You have to agree as a group that welcoming newcomers is a strength, not a weakness. Once that cultural foundation is there, scaffolding becomes the natural way people show up, even if the technique isn’t well practiced to start. But it's not a technique you're forcing, it's just how you talk to each other. Belonging Through Connection When someone asks a question and gets a thoughtful answer - when they're met with curiosity instead of judgment - they feel seen. They feel like they might belong. And that changes everything about how they learn, how they engage, and how they eventually help the next person who walks in confused. Belonging isn't something you earn through suffering. It's something you're given through attention. And the quality of our conversations - how we scaffold for each other - is what creates it. But it all starts with a culture that says yes to people before it says no. If this resonates, it's probably because you've felt it on both sides. You've been the newcomer who got shut down. And if you're honest, you've probably been the one doing the shutting down too. The good news is that conversations are where culture actually changes. Not in policy documents or team meetings. In the small moments when someone asks a question and you decide how to answer it. If you work on a product team and you're wondering whether your culture is set up to have the right conversations with customers, let’s talk. That's exactly what help teams understand and improve. **Title:** Have You Ever Seen a $1.5 Billion Document? I Helped Write One.
**Type:** Article
**Likes:** 23

**Content:**

Years ago, I was part of a small internal team at LEGO. Our job was to establish the strategic future of the LEGO Community program. Don’t get me wrong, we weren’t there to develop “community”. Community already existed, but for the first time the company was ready and willing to put resources behind creating a deep and powerful community strategy to integrate community for all ages into all parts of the business. I was part of a 10 person team who spent months working through this strategy. That work turned into LEGO's first community strategy. It was not a flashy project. There was no big launch. That document is the spark of about $1.5 billion in annual revenue from LEGO's 18+ product line. While that strategy focused on all ages and types of LEGO fans, today I wanted to share more about how this document became (at least) $1.5 billion in revenue. The story most people tell is wrong (or at least incomplete) Every time LEGO's adult revenue comes up, the explanation always sounds familar: adults are nostalgic, adults have more money, LEGO realized this and made premium sets and raised the price ceiling, and now they buy like crazy. None of that is wrong; it’s just shallow. It treats adult demand like it was just sitting there waiting to be found, as if LEGO needed the right product and the right price point to flip a switch. That's not what happened. Not by a mile. Or kilometer, since we’re talking about a Danish company. You have to remember that at this time, adult fans didn’t bring their creations to work and proudly display them on their desks. That’s no big deal nowadays, but back then it was a bold move that would cause many raised eyebrows. Now LEGO is treated like art and welcomed by many people. This was not the environment I was existing in. What we were actually trying to do When our team was doing this strategy work, we weren't using words like "adult segment" or "lifetime value expansion." Those frameworks came later. The question in front of us was more basic than that. We wanted to understand what it would mean to treat fans as participants in the brand, not just as an audience to market to. And we wanted to build structures that would let us actually hear from them directly. That eventually led to things like the LEGO Ambassador Network and a number of event support programs. At the time, these weren't framed as revenue engines. They were ways to get closer to the people who already cared and to stop guessing about what mattered to them. The LEGO Ambassador Network gave us direct lines to passionate, knowledgeable fans who could tell us what the community was thinking and feeling. Some of that paid off directly. We saw our LEGO Direct exclusive kits make money quickly, even though the pot of money wasn’t as much as the traditional mass market products. But a lot of the value was harder to measure. Fans felt heard, they stayed engaged, they told other people, and they helped to change the way the world thought about the “toy” in lieu of a “creative medium”. They deepened their relationship with the brand in ways that don't show up cleanly in a quarterly report but absolutely show up over time. But something else happened that I don't think any of us fully appreciated in the moment. We helped make being an adult LEGO fan visible and exciting. Before that, it was mostly a private thing. You might have a table in a spare room, a few bins of bricks, maybe a friend or two who knew you were into it. But there wasn't a shared identity, and there definitely wasn't a sense that the company itself saw you. You didn’t feel like an artist as much as a closet hobbyist. When it becomes part of who you are At some point, the label AFOL, which stands for Adult Fan of LEGO, stopped being a niche term and started acting like a real identity. One you didn’t hide, you tattooed on your arm. Literally. The first red LEGO brick I saw tattooed on AFOLs shoulder made me realize there was something shifting. That shift is easy to miss if you're looking at it from a spreadsheet. When something becomes part of how you see yourself, your behavior changes in ways that are both obvious and hard to measure. You spend more time on it. You care more about the quality of the experience. You look for ways to go deeper. You stop thinking about it as consumption and start thinking about it as expression. And you want to share it with absolutely everyone. Perhaps most importantly… you don't age out of it. This was insanely significant for a toy business. Kids grow up and move on. We called it the “Dark Ages”. For a kids toy company, they were constantly working to find new customers. Fill the pipeline from scratch with a limited window of opportunity. Community disrupted that pattern. It didn't just keep people around longer. It gave them a real reason to stay and to pull others in. The community was the strategy. The conversations made it possible. Looking back, the LEGO community strategy was exactly what it said it was. Community was the goal. Building real, lasting relationships with fans at every age and stage was the point. But community strategy without conversation is just org charts and programs. What made ours work was that we stayed in constant, direct contact with the people we were building it for. Conversations were how we understood what the community actually needed. They were how we validated whether our ideas were landing. And they were how we built credibility inside the company when we needed to push for something new, like the idea that adults deserved products designed specifically for them. When I stood in front of LEGO leadership and said the adult fan market was bigger than anyone realized, I wasn't presenting survey data. I was sharing what thousands of real people had told me directly. That's a different kind of argument. It's harder to dismiss. The five pillars I now teach through the Customer Conversation Compass came directly out of this experience. We had to figure out what we were missing by not talking to fans consistently, that's Conversation Debt. We had to build systems for how and when those conversations happened, that's Conversation Architecture. We had to get better at asking the right questions and actually hearing the answers, that's Conversation Tuning. We had to find ways to get those insights to the people inside LEGO who needed them, that's Conversation Amplification. And we had to turn what we learned into decisions and action, that's Conversation Traction. We didn't have those names back then. But we were doing all five things, because we had to. There was no other way to do the community strategy well. That's the through line from that document to $1.5 billion. A community strategy that was grounded in real conversations, structured to keep learning over time, and connected to the people inside the company who could act on what we heard. The document created the strategy. The conversations made it worth $1.5 billion. **Title:** Eleven Madison Park: The Whiskey Cart
**Type:** Article

**Content:**

I've been lucky enough to eat at some truly amazing places in my life. But none left a lasting impression like Eleven Madison Park (EMP) in New York. We had 10 courses of stunning food, perfectly paired drinks, and service that felt effortless from start to finish. Honestly, I can't remember any specific details about the food or drinks. I honestly can’t remember any specific details about the food, drinks, or service. It was off the charts good; my lack of specific memories doesn’t mean it wasn’t memorable. What I do remember, vividly, is the whiskey cart. I was part of a large group and we had our own private dining room. At the end of our meal, the waiter rolled in a beautiful manual coffee maker and offered us these high end, hand-crafted coffees. Sadly, I'm not a coffee drinker. How Will Pattison built a 12,000+ member online community on brutal honesty, fierce standards, and the radical willingness to lose members he never wanted in the first place. Will Pattison will be the first to tell you that Scale Modelers Critique Group (SMCG) is not a success story in any conventional sense. No explosive growth. No viral moment. No algorithm-friendly engagement strategy. Since its founding in the fall of 2015, SMCG has grown to around 12,000 members and largely stayed there. By the metrics most community managers chase, that would read as stagnation. Will sees it differently. And after spending time talking with him about how he runs the group, I think he might be onto something the rest of us have been missing. A Hole in the Market SMCG started with a clear-eyed read on a gap. As Will tells it, the scale modeling community on social media was overwhelmingly built around what he calls "attaboy culture" -- people posting their finished tanks and planes and receiving applause, full stop. Nothing wrong with that. But for the people who were genuinely obsessed with getting better, there was nowhere to go. Will describes the hobby in terms of a bell curve. At the bottom, people who are just starting out or who simply don't care much about deep improvement, just getting things completed as they learn. In the big middle, people who have reached a comfortable level and are satisfied there. And at the right-hand tail, the obsessives; the people who never stop wanting to push their skill forward, who treat mastery as its own reward. SMCG was built for that last group. "We felt like there was a hole in the market," Will told me. "There wasn't a Facebook group, or really any social media hangout, for scale model makers who really wanted to elevate their level of skill." The name "Scale Modelers Critique Group" turned out to be a double-edged decision. Will admits he'd probably choose differently if he could go back. The word "critique" makes some people assume that's all the group does, when in reality it covers technique, products, community discussion, and plenty of banter. Worse, it acts as an accidental filter: people who aren't confident in their work see the name and self-select out before they even look inside. But here's the twist: Will thinks that self-filtering might actually be working in their favor. By targeting the right-hand side of the bell curve, they've built a group that tends to plateau at the natural saturation point for highly motivated, skill-focused hobbyists. "We never cared about quantity," he says. "When we first conceived the idea, I thought we might have 500 members if we were lucky." Anarchy with an Iron Fist The phrase Will uses to describe his community management approach is, as he puts it, one he learned somewhere else entirely: the long-defunct Joe Rogan message board. It was a place he describes as "the Wild West." He spent a couple of years there and credits it with teaching him how to moderate. The strategy is this: treat members as adults. Let conversations run. Allow the community room to breathe and self-express. And then, when certain lines are crossed, act without hesitation and without apology. "We want to treat people as adults and allow them to express themselves in whatever way they see fit," Will explains. "As long as certain lines are never crossed. And if those lines are crossed, we will act with extreme prejudice." What surprised me most in talking with Will was his complete indifference to losing members. Most community managers I know carry a background anxiety about attrition; every ban/removal feels like a small defeat. Will's attitude is the opposite. People have left SMCG complaining that it's too controlling. His response, essentially: good. "What we see in other groups is not enough control. Not nearly enough." The willingness to lose the wrong members in order to protect the culture of the right ones is, in his view, not a bug in the strategy. It's the strategy. The Rule That Actually Works Will has a strong opinion about community rules, and it starts with what he considers the most useless rule in existence: "Don't be a dick." "It gives anybody who's upset the opportunity to decide that somebody's been a dick," he says. "And in the scale modeling community, there's more than a few people who are easily offended." A rule that broad becomes a weapon in the wrong hands, and a shield for the people who most need to be held accountable. SMCG's primary written rule is different: don't make it personal. Play the ball, not the man. It sounds simple, but Will enforces it at a level of specificity most groups never reach. It's not just about outright insults. His team watches for the subtler moves too: the passive-aggressive phrases that carry an edge, the tone under the surface of an otherwise innocuous comment. He gave me an example: the phrase "I’ve never had a problem with that issue." On the surface, it might be innocent. But in context, it's often something else: a way of implying that someone else's struggle reflects a personal failing. Will's take: "On the 50% chance that it's actually condescending and gatekeeping, we're just not going to let it take root." To help catch these patterns, the group uses word alerts. Certain phrases trigger a notification so that nothing slips through. And beyond the written rules, there are a handful of unwritten ones -- cultural artifacts, almost -- that exist more to maintain group identity than to prevent real harm. Will laughs about putting people on a brief timeout for saying "hairy stick" (paintbrush) or "bad boy," two phrases that just happen to irritate him. They don't do damage in the world. But they create a kind of in-group language, a shared set of references that bonds the community together. "It creates opportunities for camaraderie," he says. "It's embedded in the group culture. In many online groups, especially photography communities, the norm is tightly managed: critique is only given when explicitly requested. The thinking is that it protects members from unwanted criticism. SMCG runs on the opposite assumption. If you're in the group and you post your work, feedback is fair game. You don't have to ask for it. You're not expected to give permission. "The culture is: be prepared for it," Will says. "If you don't want it, either don't post or don't stay." This connects back to who the group is actually built for. The right-hand side of the bell curve: the people who are genuinely driven to improve and don't need a comfort wall around their work. They want contact with an honest reaction. And they want it while the work is still in process, not after the model is complete and on the display shelf. His philosophy: "If you want to produce good work, don't fall in love with your own work." The Culture That Runs Itself In the early days, Will read every comment in the group. Every single one. When someone posts unhelpful feedback like the one-line dismissal or the lazy "looks great!" that teaches nobody anything other members push back. Not the member count or the engagement rate, but the moment when the standards you've spent years building become something the community enforces for you. He acts as the enforcer when needed, without hesitation, because he views that as the job. "I will act as the authority with no hesitation," he says. "But I do not try to act as the leader. If I'm going to flex, it's going to be with a very specific purpose, and that's generally just to uphold the standards of the group. It's not about me self-identifying as the boss." That the self-selection is real. That when you build something for the right-hand tail of the bell curve, the right-hand tail finds you. His one quiet regret: the reach isn't wider. Not for his sake. For the people still sitting in "attaboy" groups getting applause when what they actually need is someone to tell them the mold line is showing. "I think we could do more good," he said. That's the thing about building with genuine standards. You stop thinking about your community and start thinking about who hasn't found it yet.
