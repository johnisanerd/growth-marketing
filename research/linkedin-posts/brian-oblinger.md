**Title:** I Built An AI Version Of Myself. Here's What I Learned.
**Type:** Article
**Likes:** 77

**Content:**

A practical guide to turning decades of expertise into an AI knowledge base that drives efficiency, scaleability, and sustainability The Problem With Being An Expert 25+ years of leading teams, consulting, speaking, and advising companies leaves you with a lot of accumulated knowledge. It also leaves you with a difficult problem. That knowledge lives in podcast episodes, presentations, email threads, client proposals, LinkedIn posts, and Slack — a significant chunk that exists only inside your head. Every time a client asks a question you've answered dozens of times before, you're trying to find the last time you answered it. Every content piece is a re-excavation of frameworks you've already built five times. Every bit of knowledge pulls from sources scattered across a dozen tools and folders. If you're a knowledge worker of any kind, this is your hidden inefficiency — hours spent hunting, collating, and reconstructing knowledge that already exists. It may not seem like that big of a deal until you stop and add it all up. A bunch of little ones make a big one. AI to the rescue!(?) The Idea: A Living, Agentic Version of Me The goal is to build a structured, AI-readable archive of everything I know, how I think, how I write, and how I work that I can prompt to get comprehensive answers and take action on my behalf. I call it Digital Brian. I started with three targeted outcomes: A library that mirrors my knowledge, skills, style, and brands so that recall is instant instead of a long manual process Automation of repetitive work like email management, content production, client proposals, and recurring tasks to free-up capacity for higher-value delivery A single primary interface for as much of my work as possible — fewer UIs, less context-switching, and greater output To be clear: authenticity and trust are the foundation of everything I do. My accumulated knowledge, business acumen, hands-on experience, and judgment are what people look to me for, and that's not changing. Every minute freed from mundane repetition is a minute reallocated to better thinking, sharper advice, and better outcomes for those in my care. Digital Brian is an accelerator, not a replacement for Human Brian. Getting Started: Building the Infrastructure The technical foundation is simpler than it sounds. AI models like Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini read and recall structured text files efficiently (markdown is the format of choice, denoted by the .md file extension), especially when organized in a container with clear instructions. The underlying approach is called RAG — Retrieval Augmented Generation — which means the AI references your library to inform its responses rather than generating from nothing. Here's the folder structure I built to serve as the architecture of my library: Brand — Visual identity, logos, color systems, and usage guidelines for each of my properties (Brian Oblinger, Community Strategy Academy, In Before The Lock) Content — Archives of every LinkedIn post, article, podcast episode, and public presentation I've produced Knowledge — Everything I've learned about community strategy, customer marketing, and CX across 25 years of hands-on delivery, client work, podcast conversations, and course development Positioning — Value propositions, service descriptions, ICP definitions, proof points Style — How I write emails, posts, longform articles, and proposals; how I structure and deliver presentations The daunting part was starting. I have decades of digital assets — podcast transcripts, course slide decks, keynote videos, emails, documents, and social content. Using Claude Cowork, I uploaded files in batches and called services via Connectors (Gmail, Google Drive, Slack, and more), with specific instructions about what each output should contain. Connecting to various sources of my knowledge helped expedite the process. The two heaviest lifts: transcribing and archiving 100+ In Before The Lock podcast episodes and 25 Community Strategy Academy courses. I also sampled years of sent emails and Slack messages, scraped six years of LinkedIn posts, and pulled documents from Google Drive. Here's a selection of the real prompts: Please create a folder called "IB4TL MP3s" inside of the Episodes folder and fill it with all of the .mp3 files from the subfolders. Leave the original files in place and place copied/duplicated files into the new folder. Create audio transcripts for each episode in .md format. Enrich with information from ib4tl.fm with each corresponding episode show notes Sample my sent emails over the past several years and build STYLE_brianoblinger-email.md. The goal is a style guide that details how I write emails, voice, tone, composition, length, and so forth to be used to write on my behalf in the future https://www.linkedin.com/company/community-strategy-academy/posts/?feedView=all&viewAsMember=true Create an archive of all posts from that page in an .md file. The goal is to have a sense of what I've posted about before, how many times, and the content so that I can plan and write future content without repeating too much Create a style .md in the same vein as STYLE_brianoblinger-linkedin.md in the Style folder Look at all of the presentations in the Events folder: 1. Create a style guide for how I craft and deliver public stage presentations. Look at structure, context, content, the composition of slides, number of slides, and so forth. The goal is to have a guide/skill that can be used for the creation of future presentations for these kinds of public talks 2. Like we did for linkedin posts and articles, create an archive of these presentations for reference 3. Create knowledge files from the contents of these presentations and add them or use them to enrich the existing files The end result: 45+ structured text files loaded into a Claude Project alongside a detailed instruction set telling the AI who I am, what I'm trying to accomplish, and how to use the library: My brain in plain text format. It's quite humbling to see 25 years of hard-earned experience distilled into a handful of plain text files. A little scary, too. But it's also clarifying — you see exactly what you know, how you think, and where the gaps are. This is a distillation of my knowledge, but not the full firehose. Don't Skip The Maintenance Building the initial library is a one-time lift. What most people overlook is that a static knowledge base is a depreciating asset. It's a snapshot of where you were, not where you're going. I built a monthly update process that automatically pulls new emails, meeting notes, documents, and LinkedIn activity into the knowledge base. It runs on the first Monday of each month and takes about five minutes to supervise. This matters more than the initial build. The compounding value of a knowledge base that stays current is an entirely different thing from one that stands still. Putting It to Work Here's where the impact shows up. Instant client advice. I can query the totality of my knowledge — frameworks, case studies, proof points, benchmarks — with a single sentence. No more hunting through old decks and transcripts. A client asks about advocacy program design; Digital Brian surfaces everything I've ever built, advised, or written on the topic in seconds: Please check my library for 3 examples of successful advocacy programs and draft a reply to [Client] Content at scale. Balancing content creation with client delivery is one of the harder parts of running a solo practice. Digital Brian builds content strategies, publishing calendars, and produces article drafts in my style and voice, informed by what I've already published so I'm not repeating myself. Of course, I review everything and make edits to ensure it meets my high quality bar and that it's authentically me. Creating a LinkedIn content strategy and drafts based on my knowledge, style, and brand. Designing graphics for social posts and promotional purposes. Proposals in seconds. Building a Statement of Work used to mean hunting through previous proposals and cobbling together the scope. Now I describe the engagement and Digital Brian produces a production-ready draft pulling from every proposal I've ever written and prices it accordingly: Build a draft SOW for [Client]. Include strategy optimization, platform selection, and ongoing advisory services. Use the meeting notes from gmail to build the executive summary Slides in a snap. Building fresh content for client presentations, keynotes, webinars, and courses is one of the most repetitive and time-consuming tasks in my line of work. It's also the one where context is critical to delivering tailored guidance. With a simple prompt, I can generate an incredible first draft on which to build final deliverables: Building slides using 25+ years of topical knowledge, context, and presentation style. Code. I recently refreshed brianoblinger.com with updated positioning, messaging, and success stories. There were components that I wanted to add that my hosting platform doesn't offer and my skills in this area are limited, so I vibe coded them: The new vibe-coded interactive services component on my website. Consistency across everything. My voice, positioning, brand, and style are more coherent across all outputs. A united Brian on all fronts. A sample from STYLE_discovery-strategy-docs.md. This is used to enforce consistency across all docs of this type. Let's Be Real About Limitations Most content I've seen on this topic skips the honest parts. I want to be clear about the limitations I encountered: Integration The Connectors and browser tools are genuinely useful but have real gaps. Google Drive search currently indexes Docs but not Slides — a meaningful constraint for someone who lives in slide decks. Browser automation handles scraping well but struggles with complex sequences like scheduling posts. I expect both to improve quickly as the tooling matures. Agency True autonomous operation isn't there yet. Background scheduling without manual invocation requires some...interesting...workarounds. This is a real constraint on the path to full agentic operation. Design Graphic design and composition currently lag behind more deterministic tasks like writing and coding. Even when equipped with extensive style guides, concrete examples, and abundant patience, coaxing AI systems into generating precisely what I want is frustrating. Admittedly, I probably care 10x more about this than anyone consuming those assets. Usage I burned through my daily and weekly usage allotment fairly quickly on the $20/month Pro plan during the infrastructure build-out phase. Parsing 100+ podcast audio files into text transcripts took a toll. I also ended up buying an additional $20 of tokens to help me get through the rest of the knowledge consolidation process. On the other hand, birthing a digital twin that can enrich all aspects of what you do for a nominal monthly fee is pretty incredible. Digital Brian cost $40 to create and $20 per month to run. Human Brian And then there's me. I'm still learning how to work most effectively with these tools. The instinct to blame the technology when output falls short is easy and usually wrong. Thinking through what you're trying to accomplish before you prompt — and iterating toward what you actually need — is a skill I'm actively building. That's the real learning curve, and it's worth acknowledging. The good news is that this is the worst these tools will ever be. Why Everyone Should Do This Now For individuals: This is the highest-leverage AI project you can take on right now. It makes you more efficient, more consistent, and demonstrably AI-forward in a way that matters to employers and clients. It's also a great forcing function for understanding how this technology actually works in your specific context. For teams: The organizational version is even more powerful. Centralize institutional knowledge. Standardize processes. Make every team member as capable as your best one. When someone leaves, their knowledge doesn't walk out with them. When someone new joins, they're productive in days instead of weeks. For leaders: Bake your standards, strategic priorities, and expectations directly into the instruction files. When everyone pulls from the same playbook consistency at scale is no longer a management problem — it's an infrastructure problem, and infrastructure is solvable. The Bigger Picture Most conversations about AI are theoretical. What might happen. What could change. What we should prepare for. Digital Brian is what it looks like in practice — a specific system, built by a specific person, solving specific problems in a specific workflow. Not magic. Infrastructure. And like any good infrastructure, the value compounds over time while the maintenance cost stays flat. If you're building something similar — or thinking about what this looks like for your team — I'd be glad to compare notes. Reach out via DM or at brianoblinger.com. **Title:** Communities, AI, & Money
**Type:** Article

**URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/communities-ai-money-brian-oblinger-47v3c
**Content:**

Summary of key points: In the age of AI, online communities remain valuable as they generate new knowledge and content, fueling AI models While traditional metrics like growth in visits and engagement may decline, communities that prioritize quality content creation will thrive Platform vendors should offer flexibility in data sharing options, allowing communities to choose between open, partially open, or closed access to AI models A general unease has set in amongst those that build online communities, the platform vendors that host them, and the drive-by hot take artists on LinkedIn that don’t know how AI actually works. What value will communities provide, they wonder, in a world where the vast knowledge contained within is destined to live life as a summarized version of itself at the end of a ChatGPT prompt? My hot take is: More valuable than ever before Communities full of passionate humans perpetually creating new content, knowledge, insights, and perspectives will continue to be pretty important Authentic Information Fuels Artificial Intelligence A core challenge we face with AI is that we haven’t educated folks about how it works at a basic level, which has led to misalignments in how people think about its potential impacts on communities. Let’s start with some basics, presented without the jargon and “you’re already behind, bro” hubris: Models are fed massive amounts of information, identify patterns, and then regurgitate that information based on their best prediction of the patterns you ask them about Responses are “generative” in the sense that they’re synthesizing a lot of data they predict are related and provide a summary, but they do not create new knowledge without being fed new information (except for when it makes things up) This means that models only “know” what we’ve told them. Humans are originators of everything and models are a mirror. It’s us. We’re the models If we slow our inputs or turn off sources of new knowledge altogether (i.e. shutting down communities), models will stagnate and become less useful for everyone Conversely, organizations that produce both quantity and quality content are more likely to be surfaced in these results, help/inform the most number of consumers, and influence the conversation on any given topic. Welcome to SEO 2.0 (I'm told this is now being referred to as "GEO," Generative Engine Optimization) If you accept the above points into your beating organic heart as being true, then it’s not hard to see that communities full of passionate humans perpetually creating new content, knowledge, insights, and perspectives will continue to be pretty important. To wit: Communities can create content at velocity and scale AI requires content at velocity and scale to “become smarter” Investing in community is a good bet in the AI age Many organizations are planning on building their own models for internal and customer-facing use cases. Having a large dataset being built and refreshed by community members will be a competitive advantage in its own right. Lastly, I believe that we are going to be AI’d to death and will be desperately looking for authentic connection with real people wherever we can find it. For that reason alone, I’m as bullish as I’ve ever been on communities. Growth is no longer synonymous with success The Times They Are A-Changin’ In addition to the changes in technology and investments, our mindsets around what constitutes “success” of online communities need updating. Businesses have traditionally measured success of communities by building for growth in visits, visitors, and engagement metrics. However, we are starting to see declines in traffic to communities and content pages, almost certainly due to information being surfaced directly in summarized search results. To boot, we have almost no insight today into how and how many times our content is being surfaced via these results, and so organizations are predictably freaking out. While I do believe that the tools and insights will come in time, we should take the opportunity to wrap our heads around new realities: Growth is no longer synonymous with success Helping someone somewhere else is still a success Motivating, incentivizing, recognizing, and rewarding experts for long-term, sustainable creation of quality knowledge content will be one of your most important investments Communities will become the center of most marketing strategies based on the need to drive authenticity, differentiation, and volume With ability to measure direct impact severed, customer research/surveys will become the primary mechanism of understanding the value of communities Many companies are going to miss this memo, throw their hands in the air in the face of declining Unique Visitors, and conclude that community is no longer relevant. When they have no presence in the primary technologies via which life on the internet originates (search, assistants, agents), they will have deep regret and a massive hill to climb to get back into the game. Better to accept the new realities and adapt to survive. We are going to be AI’d to death and will be desperately looking for authentic connection with real people Choose Your Own Adventure Much of the above is written from the perspective of organizations that value the distribution of their supply to the masses. But, not every community is right for scraping, training, and summarization (think cancer support groups, expectant mothers, and other sensitive topics). Others may seek to acquire new members without giving away for free the value offered within (think membership communities like associations). To enable this variety of use cases, platform vendors need to build new functionality that puts the power in the hands of community builders. Broadly, there are several configurations to build around: Open, Trainable — Fully-public support, product, success, and marketing communities available to models Partially Open, Partially Trainable — Communities where, like paywalled news sites, only some information is available for the purposes of being available to models for distribution. The bulk of value requires registration/membership to view and consume Closed, Not Trainable — Communities that are private for good reason and never want their content to be used by models The second option would be net new functionality for most platforms and should become a standard offering for those that want to live in the middle ground. Communities could toggle between these options as their use cases/businesses mature. Of course, even if you choose to be closed and not trainable by 3rd party models, nothing should stop you from training your own models on your private community data. Not every community is right for scraping, training, and summarization Training Data Is The New Oil Communities that host unique and valuable content (and a large volume of it) will have a clearer path to financial upside given the option to market and sell their data to AI companies for training purposes. We’ve already seen this play out with OpenAI paying Reddit $60M for access to their data, for example. Communities could do this directly, and vendors could take a margin by acting as a facilitator of relationships with the model makers. Imagine that your platform vendor has an existing relationship with Perplexity, for example. You can opt into sharing your data in exchange for credits on your bill or a literal check, with your vendor taking a percentage of the proceeds for maintaining the integration and absorbing the server activity required for it all to work. This option won’t make sense for every community, and thoughtfulness should be applied with respect to members that feel their efforts are being sold out from under them without getting their due. Do we cut them in? Does that add risk in terms of members being classified as employees if they’re directly compensated? I don’t have all of the answers on this front, but would love to hear your thoughts. The only thing I’m certain of is that I’m wrong. The question is, how wrong? What am I missing? Do you see it playing out differently? How is your organization planning on leveraging AI and community together?

**Title:** Activate Your Community to Build Better Products and Ignite Adoption
**Type:** Article

**Content:**

While support cost deflection is the gateway for most companies to find value with online communities, product-focused communities are on the rise — and with good reason. There is tremendous value to be had by engaging your customers to better educate them about product capabilities, gather critical feedback, and leverage their collective experience to build better products through ideation. When customers understand the product better, feel that they are a partner in driving functional enhancements, and have a more active role in defining the product roadmap , your products get better and business value quickly follows. During my time in tech, we discovered that customers that visited the community regularly tended to have higher levels of satisfaction, expanded their accounts more rapidly, and used more of the available functionality. In turn, we saw our revenue and net retention rates skyrocket. Oh, and they required less hands-on support. The Open Source community has been engaging in these behaviors for years and should be lauded for the learnings that many software companies have adopted and are enjoying great success with today. You can use these same strategies to help build better products and ignite adoption among your customers. Let’s take a look at three key areas in which product-focused communities can add value to your organization, in order mirroring the path that community growth typically takes place. Fast, Focused Feedback Companies spend a lot of time, money, and resources guessing what their customers want. Instead, you should simply ask them. Having a lively community full of customers that are eager to engage puts you in a natural position to solicit feedback on their most pressing needs, overall product direction, and feature/functionality prioritization with a level of efficiency and scale that is hard to achieve by other means. If you already have a community, chances are high that they’re already discussing their pain points and how they would like to see forward progress. In addition to assessing the tone and tenor of conversations, there are opportunities to leverage your community for more formalized methods of gathering feedback — embed a survey, create an advisory group, open a formal ideation area, or invite top contributors to directly connect with Product Managers and Engineers. Your community will embrace their advisory role and provide you with insights that you never would have gained otherwise. Of course, none of this matters if you don’t do something about what you hear. The biggest mistake that companies make is not gathering the feedback with formal processes, prioritizing it in the roadmap, dedicating engineering cycles to make the product better, and closing the loop with the community at release time. Don’t be that company. Education & Enablement Many companies struggle to gain wide adoption of features and functionality in their products that drive additional revenue, satisfaction, and retention. A core reason for this is that, frankly, they don’t do a good job of educating their customers about the benefits of various features. Even when education about new features is done, it's often not framed the way customers speak. Communities force you to think and talk like a customer. Another key learning, which should be obvious, is that customers that are more informed about how your product functions and the value that it can add to their work/lives tend to utilize more features, achieve higher levels of satisfaction, and adopt new functionality faster. Community can play a central role in this formula by enabling knowledge sharing among members, presenting them with a steady stream of high-quality education and enablement materials, and rewarding them for being recognized as experts with specific functionality. Some organizations have taken this to the next level by integrating interactive learning directly into their communities. Salesforce Trailhead and Alteryx Academy are excellent examples of what can happen when you make education and enablement a core piece of the community experience and reward users for learning something new. Challenges and certifications drive competitive behaviors among your members, leading to a self-sustaining model of customers’ overall acumen and adoption of features. Communities are uniquely positioned to deliver on this experience in ways that traditional content marketing and siloed e-learning can’t because of the member-centric environment, tight integration with other community modalities, and critical mass of users that visit every day. You have the opportunity to show them something they’ve never seen before. Don’t miss it. Build Belonging At the end of the day, a product is just a product. The people that use it, what they use it for, and how it makes them feel are what makes it special. Building a community and a sense of belonging to something bigger than one’s self around your products has incredible benefits. When your customers seize the reins of personal ownership over a product that they love and that they feel loves them back, it becomes something else entirely. They will share the profound impact of your product with others, recruiting more loyal customers that are excited to help you shape the future of it. This virtuous cycle is extremely difficult to achieve without the weight of a vibrant community full of passionate supporters behind it. Expecting customers to fully embrace the many facets of your product and help you build it better with every release requires thoughtfulness, care, and attention on your part to ensure that you’re leading with value for your community members. Then you’ll see better adoption, higher satisfaction, and more eagerness to collaborate with your teams. There are many tangible strategies, tactics, and best practices that you can use to get there. When it starts to happen, though, it feels like pure magic. ## Post 5 — 2023-04-03
**Title:** The Future of Online Communities In An AI-Driven World
**Type:** Article
**Likes:** 96
**URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/future-online-communities-ai-driven-world-brian-oblinger
**Content:**

There’s a lot of buzz around AI and folks have been reaching out to ask what I believe the future of online communities looks like in light of these new innovations. It’s early and hard to know what awaits us. Here are six predictions based on what (we think) we know today. 1. Communities will get worse before they get better Right now, people are rushing to figure out how to cram AI into communities. Most use cases I’ve seen involve tacking on chat bots, automating the answering of questions, or generating massive amounts of mid-quality content. My gut tells me that many communities will abuse this technology to astroturf content, discussions, and answers which will lead to unsatisfying results for members. Metrics like Solution Rate and Time To Solution will become less relevant as the quality of the solutions decline. I suspect that owners/admins of communities will harm themselves and their members by haphazardly adding AI into the mix without fully considering the consequences in the short term. Long term, I hope to see more thoughtfulness about how it’s deployed and the value to the user. 2. Authentic, verified human connection becomes more important than ever Ironically, the explosion of AI capabilities will likely drive even more emphasis, differentiation, and value of talking to real people with insights gained through experience and intuition. That’s not to say that the contributions of AI to communities won’t be valuable, it’s that we’re going to vastly over-do it in the beginning and “AI fatigue” will set in pretty quickly with most folks. If you thought bots were annoying before, you haven’t seen anything yet. We can argue about the implementation details, but I believe that Elon and others are philosophically correct in saying that the future of social media and communities is the ability to filter by verified humans. Bots will become ubiquitous and humans will become novel/valuable. I wouldn’t be surprised at all if we find ourselves all signing up with a new utility service that specializes in verification that is then portable and integrated across the web in pursuit of discovering and engaging with real people. Talking to bots only will get old, real fast. 3. Smaller and private communities will flourish To that end, we are likely to see further pushes into small, private group chats as one prevailing form of community simply because you can control for known humans only and the content will not be indexible/trainable by LLMs. The last point will become a major factor in where people choose to participate going forward. The idea that everything you say can and will become part of a training set will not sit well with people. Privacy will become a defining characteristic and value prop for platforms. This will have a downstream net effect of there being less publicly-available, indexable, quality information in open communities and the internet as a whole. **Title:** Zen And The Art Of Calendar Management
**Type:** Article
**Likes:** 35
**URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/zen-art-calendar-management-brian-oblinger
**Content:**

Over the years, much has been made about the benefits of the “Inbox Zero” lifestyle, including from yours truly. We put a lot of time and energy into creating systems and habits to keep email from running our lives. But what about our calendars? In the always on times we live in, we should place the same level of focus and rigor into ensuring that we are the masters of our time and attention. Time is your most valuable resource. Protect it.Unlike email, the ephemeral nature of events and time make digging out of whatever hole you’ve created for yourself a more sensible proposition. You may not be able to change your immediate calendaring disaster in progress, but with a few simple changes, you can craft a better system for how your time is allotted and managed going forward. Follow along below to start taking back your time.Change Your MindsetThe primary reason that people are bad at managing their time is that they are worried that tighter control will make them appear lazy, inflexible, and not a team player. This, frankly, is bullshit. You have a responsibility to be the best, most effective version of yourself. Ensuring that you have time to complete tasks and space for thought is extremely important.Joining back-to-back meetings for nine straight hours isn’t a badge of honor that proves you’re a great worker. It’s an ineffective use of your talents and the path to burnout. It turns out that you have to actually accomplish something to be valuable, not just attend a bunch of (let’s be honest, mostly worthless) meetings.Instead of measuring your effectiveness in terms of the number of meetings you take every day, measure your effectiveness in terms of outputs and value generated. This change in mindset requires a change in how you handle your time.Set Better ExpectationsUnderstand that others aren’t going to respect boundaries for your time. If you make it available to them, they will take it. When you work and when you are available to meet are not the same thing. The first step of communicating this is to establish the hours that you are willing and available to meet.All modern calendaring applications allow you set these hours. It provides a visual representation of your set hours when viewing your calendar and may warn meeting organizers if they attempt to schedule you outside of your chosen availability.Of course, these are only passive techniques. People can still place time on your calendar regardless, but at least you’re setting better expectations.Services like Calendly take this a step further in that they do not permit someone to schedule a meeting outside of the available hours you have set. More on the benefits of these services below.Show Only Free/BusyIn corporate settings where everyone can see each others' calendars, it is common for sleuths to ask if they can book a time that’s already spoken for. Often the request will be accompanied by either an overt or explicit mention that the meeting they’re trying to schedule is more important for various reasons, leading to a round of negotiation and re-schedules. Letting everyone see exactly what’s on your calendar makes it more difficult to manage expectations and control your own time.Instead, set your access permissions to show only free/busy times to others looking at your calendar. This also aids with privacy and will enable you to block time without anyone feeling entitled to it.Calendar management services take this a step further by only showing times that you are free, reducing even further the potential to be double booked or have to debate about specific time slots. This becomes particularly effective when connected to multiple calendars (work, personal, family), whittling down the available times even further to reflect the true state of your availability.Book Shorter MeetingsMost meetings shouldn’t take an hour. Heck, most shouldn’t take 30. Reduce the default meeting duration by 5 or 10 minutes and see how it feels. You’ll be surprised what can be discussed and decided when the clock is ticking. Eventually you’ll have a great feel for how long certain types of conversations should take and can manage accordingly.With respect to Calendly, setup meeting types of specific durations and send the specific URL to the duration that you want people to book (30 minutes, for example) rather than the page with all of the options. If you let people self-select, they’ll often choose a duration that is longer than required, monopolizing even more of your time.Have A “Hard Stop”Get in the habit of responding with a firm “yes” when someone asks if you have a “hard stop.” Once you’ve given someone permission to take more of your time, it’s a lot harder to bring it to a close. You’ve also set a bad expectation for future meetings wherein your time isn’t valuable and going over by 5, 10, or 60 minutes isn’t a big deal.Give Yourself A BreakWith tools like Calendly, you can set buffers around your events to ensure that you’re not scheduled back-to-back all day long. This is particularly useful with 15 and 45 minute duration meetings that effectively book as 30 and 60 on your calendar. Use the spare time to finish your notes from the call, send a follow-up email, take a bio break, grab a snack, or think. This space will become invaluable to your workflow and contributes to a greater quality of life.No Last Minute MeetingsPreparing for meetings is an important part of being an effective worker. You can’t do that if people are dropping meetings on your calendar a few minutes prior to the start time. Calendly allows you to set a rolling threshold of availability that prevents the last minute meeting.You can also set a maximum number of each meeting type if you wish so that someone can’t drop a sixth 60 minute meeting of the day on you.Schedule Your WorkNeed to work on a presentation? Place a 90 minute event on your calendar to work on it.Want to spend time responding to all of those emails piling up? Place a 30 minute event on your calendar.Need a few free minutes before your next call? Drop a 15 minute “hold.”Accounting for time spent working in this way is important for you to make sure that you have the necessary time to get actual work done and ensures that someone can’t block you from getting things done by scheduling a meeting. Want to eat lunch every day? Create a recurring event to ensure that no one can schedule over it. Your mental health will thank you.One Thing At A TimeStop double booking yourself. You cannot be in two places at once. Multitasking is a lie.All of the above tactics should help significantly reduce this problem, but you’ll still find a way to do this if you’re not careful. Begin by resolving existing conflicts on your calendar and don’t allow any more going forward. Your day will be much more manageable when you can focus on one thing at a time.There Is “Yes” And There Is “No.” There Is No “Maybe.”Are you going or not? Stop hedging. Your calendar should be an honest reflection of reality. Accept or Decline accordingly.Let Your FOMO GoYou don’t need to be at every meeting. Really.Do Unto Others...As you’ve read through this, there were undoubtedly times when you thought of obvious exceptions and situations where being as stringent as I’ve described may not be prudent or reasonable. You’re right. There will always be situations that arise that create special conditions and circumstances and you should handle them with thoughtful adjustments to your schedule as necessary.It is important to reciprocate the care and respect you have for your own time for others’ time. Always observe The Golden Rule.Take control. Be free. Be happy.

You'll be shocked how much easier managing email is when half of them suddenly stop showing up in your inbox. Worried that you'll be missing out on valuable information like when the party starts in the Pittsburgh office (you work in the San Francisco office)? You won't. Cut it and move on. Sure, but then I'd have one more rule in the list of rules in Outlook/Gmail (be ruthless) and risk slipping back into old ways of sleuthing through them every day even if they're off in some folder I hastily threw together titled "Other." You can double or triple your efficiency very quickly. For email that you can't unsubscribe from or can automate actions that you're currently doing manually 37 times per day, create rules. You might be surprised how much of your daily mail juggling can be done automagically. Example Rules: Non-critical update emails that I should look at later, but not now: Flag for Follow-Up > Mark As Read > Move to [Folder] Bill reminder emails for auto-pay accounts: Mark As Read > Apply "Bills" Label > Archive Emails from organizations to which both my wife and I belong, but we use my address for the account: Forward to Wife Emails from my best friend: Delete (be ruthless...just kidding [but seriously]) Create a System for Your Email A single stream of emails in your inbox that are either Read or Unread is a system, but one that is prone to errors and is harder to wrangle in the long-term. It looks like this: Mail comes into Inbox Quickly scan contents to determine appropriate action Flag / Don't Flag / Unsubscribe File in appropriate folder (create a folder structure that is specific to classify your mail) Keep Inbox at 0 at all times Work out of the Flagged for Follow-Up queue When action on mail in queue complete, uncheck and watch it disappear Next. DND can be enabled manually as needed or on a set schedule. I recommend setting up a schedule that fits your lifestyle so that it works automatically each day. Set it to enable earlier than you think it should and disable later than you think it should for maximum effect. Prioritize focus and happiness over noise and distraction. Afraid you're going to miss that call from a friend or a Snapchat from a celebrity? Don't worry. You'll get to it later when the time is right for you. This is the fundamental mental shift required - you decide when your time and attention is applied. Keep It Up None of the above matters if you don't commit. This isn't a diet, it's a permanent lifestyle change. When an unwanted marketing email comes in, click unsubscribe. When you download a new app on your phone, take 15 seconds to disable its notifications. When you see something repetitive that annoys you in your Facebook feed, unfollow the account. Be ruthless. This may seem like a chore that you don't have time for, but it isn't. You're prioritizing your focus and happiness over noise and distraction. What it adds up to is freedom from digital "stuff" that used to own you. Take control. Be free. Be happy.
